=head1 How RT is organized: core concepts

Request Tracker (RT) has its own way of naming things, and a handful of ideas
carry almost the whole system. This page walks through them once, in plain
terms, so the rest of the documentation can build on a shared vocabulary. Read
it start to finish and you will have the mental model behind every task you do
in RT: what a ticket is, how tickets are grouped and moved along, who is
connected to them, and the other kinds of things RT can track.

Nothing here is a step-by-step procedure. Each idea gets a short introduction
and a link to the page that covers it in depth. If you want to jump straight
into working a ticket, start with
L<Getting started with RT|docs/user/getting_started.pod> and come back here
when a word needs unpacking.

This guide describes a typical RT configuration; your RT may be set up
differently.

=head2 How RT tracks work: the ticket

=head3 Tickets

A L<ticket|docs/glossary.pod> is the unit of work in RT. It is RT's record of
one request or task, from the moment it arrives to the moment it is done.
Everything you do in RT happens on a ticket: you create one, add to it, hand it
off, and eventually close it. A ticket carries a subject, the people involved,
the messages exchanged, and a full history of what happened along the way.

A ticket's importance does not end when the work does. After it is resolved, the
ticket stays in RT as a permanent record. It is a history of past work you can
search and reopen, a reference when a similar request comes back, the source data
behind reports on what your team handled and how long it took, and the place where
details like tracked time live. This is why a resolved ticket is still worth
finding, and why searching and reporting matter as much as working a ticket does
in the moment.

The information stored on a ticket, its subject, dates, priority, and the rest,
is covered in L<Ticket metadata|docs/ticket_metadata.pod>.

=head3 Queues

A L<queue|docs/glossary.pod> is how RT groups tickets. Think of it as a shared
inbox for a team, a product, or a project: related tickets live together in one
queue, and the people who work them share it. Every ticket belongs to exactly
one queue. A support team might keep customer email in one queue and internal
IT requests in another, so each group sees its own work without wading through
everyone else's.

A queue is also where much of RT's setup lives. Each queue carries its own set
of statuses (its lifecycle), its own watchers, its own custom fields, and its
own automation. That is why two queues in the same RT can behave differently:
they are configured separately.

=head3 Statuses and lifecycles

A ticket's L<status|docs/glossary.pod> says where it stands. In RT's default
setup a ticket usually moves from C<new> (just arrived) to C<open> (someone is
working it) to C<resolved> (done). Other statuses exist, such as C<stalled> for
work that is blocked. Status is what lets a team see at a glance what still needs
attention and what is waiting on someone else, and it is one of the most common
things to search and report on, as in "show me everything still open in my queue".

The list of statuses a ticket can have, and the moves allowed between them, is
called a L<lifecycle|docs/glossary.pod>. Each queue uses one lifecycle, and
administrators can define their own. This is the reason the exact statuses in
your RT may differ from the ones above: the statuses you see, and the order they
follow, come from the lifecycle your administrator set for that queue, not from
a fixed list. When a page in this documentation names a status like C<resolved>,
it means the default lifecycle. For how lifecycles are defined, see
L<Ticket Lifecycles|docs/customizing/lifecycles.pod>.

=head3 People and roles

People connect to a ticket through L<roles|docs/glossary.pod>. Every ticket has
four built-in roles: the B<Owner> (the one person responsible for working it),
the B<Requestors> (the people the ticket is for), and B<Cc> and B<AdminCc>
(others kept in the loop). You can see them in the B<People> box on a ticket.

Roles do more than label people. They decide who gets email about the ticket:
Requestors, Ccs, and AdminCcs are the ticket's L<watchers|docs/glossary.pod>, the
people RT notifies as the ticket changes. Roles can also carry rights. Because a
role is a relationship to the ticket rather than a fixed name, an administrator
can grant a permission to whoever holds a role: for example, allowing only the
ticket's Owner to reply to it or change it. The right person's access then follows
the ticket as it is handed off, with no one editing permissions by hand. For a
fuller account of the built-in roles, and how administrators add custom ones, see
L<Roles in RT|docs/custom_roles.pod>.

=head3 History and transactions

RT records every change to a ticket. Each recorded change, a reply, a comment, a
status change, a handoff, is a L<transaction|docs/glossary.pod>, and a ticket's
history is simply its list of transactions in order. Replies and comments are
transactions too, which is why they show up in the history alongside the field
changes.

Because RT keeps the whole history, a ticket stays a complete record of what
happened, who did it, and when. Nothing is quietly overwritten.

Many other objects in RT record history as well and for the same reason. As
changes are made, the system keeps a running log of what the change was,
who did it, and when.

=head3 Custom fields

RT tickets come with a standard set of fields, but every organization tracks
something extra. A L<custom field|docs/glossary.pod> captures that data: a
category, a piece of hardware, a contract number, whatever your team needs beyond
the built-in fields. Custom fields come in many types, so each piece of data is
stored in the shape that fits it: a date for an approval date, a dropdown for an
issue type, a text box for free-form notes, and more. Keeping this information in
its own field, rather than buried in the ticket's messages, is what lets you
search and report on it later, for example listing every open ticket tied to one
contract number. An administrator defines the custom fields for a queue, and they
then appear on its tickets.

=head3 Links

A L<link|docs/glossary.pod> records a relationship between one ticket and
something else, so related work stays visibly connected instead of scattered
across separate tickets. A ticket can depend on another, refer to it, or sit in a
parent-and-child relationship. Links are not limited to other tickets: a ticket
can also link to an asset, an article, or another object, and to a plain web URL.
This is how you tie a fix to the several tickets asking for it, or point a ticket
at the piece of equipment it concerns. Both custom fields and links are covered in
L<Ticket metadata|docs/ticket_metadata.pod>.

=head3 Scrips: automation at a glance

Much of what feels automatic in RT comes from L<scrips|docs/glossary.pod>. A
scrip is a rule made of two parts: a condition (when should this run?) and an
action (what should happen?). On every update to a ticket, RT checks the scrip's
condition, and if it is true, runs the action. A common example: when someone
replies, email the Requestor.

Notifications are the most visible thing scrips do, but not the limit of what
they can do. A scrip's action can do essentially anything a program can: call an
outside system's API, set a custom field, or even open one or more new tickets in
response to what just happened. This is a large part of how a team makes RT fit
its own process rather than the reverse. You do not set up scrips as an everyday
user, but it helps to know that the emails and updates you see are usually a scrip
doing its job, and that much more than notifying is possible. Administrators can
add their own; for the full picture, see
L<Scrip Conditions & Actions|docs/customizing/scrip_conditions_and_action.pod>.

=head2 More than tickets

Tickets are the heart of RT, but they are not the only thing it tracks. Two
other kinds of records work much like tickets and share RT's custom fields and
permissions.

=head3 Assets and catalogs

RT can track things as well as requests. An L<asset|docs/glossary.pod> is a
record of something your organization owns or manages: a laptop, a software
license, a piece of equipment. Assets let you tie the thing to the tickets
about it, so its history travels with it.

Assets are grouped into L<catalogs|docs/glossary.pod> the way tickets are
grouped into queues. For an overview see L<Assets|docs/assets.pod>, and to set
it up see the L<Assets tutorial|docs/customizing/assets/tutorial.pod>.

=head3 Articles and classes

An L<article|docs/glossary.pod> is reusable text: a canned answer, a set of
instructions, a snippet of standard wording. Articles help staff answer faster
and more consistently, and they can be dropped straight into a reply. Together
they form a small knowledge base inside RT.

Articles are organized into L<classes|docs/glossary.pod>, which group related
articles the way a queue groups tickets (just as catalogs group assets).
Answering faster is their most common use, but articles do more than supply
canned text: they can also hold information that powers other RT features,
such as Process Articles and the article links shown on dashboards. For an
overview, see L<Introduction to Articles|docs/customizing/articles_introduction.pod>.

=head2 People and permissions

=head3 Privileged and unprivileged users, and the two interfaces

RT users come in two kinds, and the kind decides which interface they see. This
is worth understanding early, because it explains why the same RT can look
completely different to two people. A L<privileged user|docs/glossary.pod> is a
staff member or agent, someone who works tickets. They get the full RT interface:
queues, search, dashboards, and the ticket tools this documentation describes. In
our examples, C<staff1> is a privileged user.

An L<unprivileged user|docs/glossary.pod> is usually a requester: someone who
submits requests and follows their own, but does not work other people's tickets.
They see a simpler interface called L<Self Service|docs/glossary.pod>, which shows
just their own tickets and a way to create more. This keeps the full staff
interface out of the way of people who only need to ask for help and check on
their requests. In our examples, C<user1> is an unprivileged user. Which interface
you land in after login depends on which kind of user your account is.

With configuration, an administrator can extend an unprivileged user's rights so
they see more than just their own tickets, for example letting everyone at a
company see all of that company's tickets. Even then, the interface stays the
simpler Self Service view: broader visibility does not turn an unprivileged user
into a staff user.

=head3 Rights and permissions

L<Rights|docs/glossary.pod> control what each user can see and do in RT: view a
ticket, reply to it, change its owner, create tickets in a queue, and so on.
Nothing in RT is visible or editable to everyone by default; each ability is a
right that someone grants.

Rights are granted to people through the groups and roles they belong to, and
they can apply across all of RT or only within a single queue (or catalog, or
class). This is the reason a button, a queue, or a whole feature can be there
for a colleague but missing for you: your account may not have that right. If
you expect to see something and cannot, you are not doing anything wrong; ask
your team lead, who can check what your account is allowed to do. Rights are set
up by administrators, so how they are arranged is a question for whoever manages
your RT.

=head1 Is Request Tracker right for your team?

Every organization has a stream of requests coming in: from customers, from
coworkers, from its own systems raising alerts. Someone has to notice each
one, decide who handles it, and see it through. When those requests live
scattered across personal inboxes, sticky notes, and spreadsheets that only
one person keeps up to date, work gets missed, credit and blame land on
whoever remembers best, and nobody can say for certain what is still open,
who owns it, or how long it has been waiting.

Request Tracker (RT) exists to close that gap: every request becomes a
tracked, visible piece of work instead of a message sitting in someone's
inbox. The sections below walk through the situations teams most often bring
to RT (help desks, IT, security, change management, equipment and
agreements, HR), each opening with the problem in the user's own words and
the RT capability that answers it.

=head2 Help desk and customer support

Customers reach you by email, by phone, through a form, however they can,
and every one of those has to turn into a real answer. Miss one and you have
an unhappy customer; answer the same question for the tenth time this month
and you have burned an hour you did not need to spend.

RT turns incoming support email straight into L<tickets|docs/glossary.pod>,
RT's record of a single request from the moment it arrives to the moment it
is done, so nothing depends on one person's inbox or memory. Customers who
want to check on their own request, without calling anyone, can use
L<Self Service|docs/glossary.pod>, a simpler view limited to their own
tickets. When your team answers the same question repeatedly, a shared
library of L<Articles|docs/glossary.pod> lets staff search for a canned
answer and drop it straight into a reply instead of retyping it; see
L<Introduction to Articles|docs/customizing/articles_introduction.pod>. And
if you have made a commitment (a response within four hours, nothing left
untouched overnight), RT's L<SLA|docs/glossary.pod> feature assigns that
target to the ticket and tracks whether you are meeting it; see
L<Service Level Agreements|docs/customizing/sla.pod>.

The result is a support operation where nothing falls through, customers can
get a status update without asking anyone, and your team answers faster and
more consistently than any one person's memory would allow.

=head2 One system for every team

IT tracks its work one way, HR another, facilities on paper, and the people
who need to see across all of them (a manager, an operations lead) cannot,
because every team built its own system in isolation.

In RT, each team gets its own L<queue|docs/glossary.pod>: a container for
its tickets with its own statuses, its own fields, and its own automation,
so a support queue and an HR queue can each look and behave the way that
team needs without touching each other's setup. Someone with the right
access can search or report across every queue at once, so leadership gets
one picture of what is outstanding across the whole organization instead of
several disconnected ones. Because each queue is configured on its own,
adding a second team, or a tenth, never means redoing the first: most
organizations start with one team on RT and add the next once the first is
running well.

One system, many teams, each working the way it already does, with the full
picture visible to whoever needs it. This is the strongest reason to bring
RT in even if you only have one urgent case in mind today: the platform you
set up for a help desk is the same platform your next team can move onto
tomorrow.

=head2 Security and incident response

Reports come in faster than anyone can look at them one at a time: a
phishing report, a vulnerability scan result, a user account behaving
strangely. When ten of those turn out to be the same incident, someone needs
to notice that before ten people start investigating it separately, and
everyone with a stake (security, IT, sometimes legal) needs to see the same
current picture.

Each alert source can feed its own queue, so security reports do not mix in
with everything else, and RT's automation, L<scrips|docs/glossary.pod>, can
tag a report, notify the right people, or even open a follow-up ticket the
moment one arrives, before anyone has read it. When several reports turn out
to be the same incident, L<linking|docs/glossary.pod> them together keeps
the investigation in one place instead of scattered across separate
tickets, and RT's full L<transaction|docs/glossary.pod> history means every
action taken during the incident (who did what and when) stays on the
record afterward.

If incident response is a full-time discipline for your team, a security
operations center or a CERT, it is worth knowing about RTIR (RT for
Incident Response): a separate product, an L<extension|docs/glossary.pod>
built on RT and purpose-built for that workflow, using RT as the base.
Learn more at L<requesttracker.com/rtir|https://requesttracker.com/rtir>.
Either way, the goal is the same: nothing gets triaged twice, nothing gets
lost in the noise, and the record survives the incident.

=head2 IT operations and change management

Requests pile up faster than your team can work them (an outage next to a
password reset next to an equipment request), and when everything feels
urgent, telling what actually is urgent, and making sure it does not sit
forgotten while something louder takes over, is the whole job.

Every ticket carries a priority, and RT can automatically raise it the
closer a ticket gets to its due date while it remains unresolved, so aging
work surfaces itself instead of waiting for someone to notice; see
"Escalating Priority" in L<Automating Tasks in RT|docs/automating_rt.pod>.
When a change should not proceed without sign-off (a configuration change, a
policy exception), RT can create an L<approval|docs/glossary.pod>: a ticket
that gates another ticket's progress until an approver accepts or rejects
it. Approvals are tickets themselves, so approvers work them like any other
ticket, and the full record of who approved what, and when, becomes part of
the permanent history; see L<RT Approvals|docs/customizing/approvals.pod>.

The result is a queue where priority reflects reality instead of whoever
complained loudest, and changes go ahead only once the right person has
actually said yes.

=head2 Assets and agreements

You know there is a pile of laptops, software licenses, and vendor contracts
out there somewhere, but the only record of most of them is a spreadsheet
nobody keeps current, and by the time someone notices a support contract has
lapsed, it already has.

RT can track the things themselves, not just the requests about them: an
L<asset|docs/glossary.pod> is a record of a laptop, a license, a contract,
anything your organization owns or manages, grouped into
L<catalogs|docs/glossary.pod> the way tickets are grouped into queues; see
L<Assets|docs/assets.pod>. Link an asset to the tickets about it and its
repair and request history travels with it, so the next person who touches
that laptop can see everything that happened to it. Give a contract or a
license a L<custom field|docs/glossary.pod> for its renewal or expiration
date, and you can save a search that finds everything coming due and put it
on a L<dashboard|docs/glossary.pod> your team checks, or have RT email it to
them on a schedule, so a lapse does not sneak up on anyone; see
L<Subscriptions and Feeds|docs/subscriptions_feeds.pod>.

Equipment and agreements stop being a mystery kept in one person's
spreadsheet and become a searchable record that raises its hand before a
lapse becomes a problem.

=head2 HR and internal processes

Onboarding a new hire touches five different people: IT provisions
accounts, facilities issues a badge, a manager signs off on equipment, HR
files the paperwork. Miss a handoff and the new hire shows up with no
laptop, no badge, or no system access. Offboarding is the same list run in
reverse, except now every step matters for security, not just first-day
smoothness, and you may need to show later that each one actually happened.

RT lets one ticket depend on another: a ticket cannot be resolved while it
still depends on unresolved tickets, so you can require account
provisioning, equipment issue, and paperwork to finish in whatever order the
process actually needs, and nothing gets marked done out of turn; see
L<Ticket metadata|docs/ticket_metadata.pod>. Every step along the way (who
did what and when) is recorded automatically and never quietly edited, so
the finished checklist is also a record you can produce later if a process
needs to be shown to have happened, not just remembered.

An onboarding or offboarding process becomes a checklist that enforces its
own order and leaves proof of itself behind, instead of a series of emails
and hallway reminders.

=head2 Why one system covers this much ground

Help desk, multi-department platform, security response, change
management, assets, HR process: these look like different problems, but RT
answers all of them with the same handful of parts. A queue gives every
kind of work its own space. L<Roles|docs/glossary.pod> and
L<watchers|docs/glossary.pod> track who is connected to each piece of work
and make sure the right people hear about it. L<Lifecycles|docs/glossary.pod>
and scrips encode the steps a process must follow and automate what would
otherwise be manual busywork. A complete transaction history means nothing
is ever quietly lost. And email sits at the core of all of it, since most
requests still arrive that way. Combine those parts differently and you get
a help desk, an IT service desk, an incident response system, a
change-approval process, an asset registry, or an HR workflow, all running
on the same underlying platform.

The same parts support plenty of other uses as well: project management,
bug tracking, sales lead tracking, even a personal to-do list.

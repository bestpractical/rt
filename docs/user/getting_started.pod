=head1 Getting started with RT: from your first login to your first resolved ticket

This tutorial walks you through your first day in Request Tracker (RT), the tool your
team uses to receive, track, and answer requests. By the end you will have logged in,
found your way around, and taken one request from a new ticket to a resolved one.

A L<ticket|docs/glossary.pod> is RT's record of a single request or task, from the
moment it arrives to the moment it is done. Working in RT means creating tickets,
replying to them, and resolving them. That full cycle is what you will practice here.

You do not need to have used RT before. You do need a login and a running RT; the
L</Before you start> section lists everything.

This guide describes a typical RT configuration; your RT may be set up
differently.

=head2 Before you start

You need three things:

=over 4

=item *

B<The web address of your RT.> Your administrator gives you this. It looks like
C<https://rt.example.com>.

=item *

B<A username and password.> Your administrator creates your account. (Some
organizations use a company sign-on instead. If yours does, login happens
automatically: you might see a brief redirect, and then you are on your homepage.)

=item *

B<A queue to work in.> A L<queue|docs/glossary.pod> is a container that groups
related tickets, much like an inbox a whole team shares. Ask your team lead which
queue your team works in; this tutorial uses one called C<General>, RT's default.

=back

=head2 Log in to RT

=over 4

=item 1

Open your RT web address in a browser. RT shows the login page.

=item 2

Type your B<Username> and B<Password>.

=item 3

Click B<Login>.

=back

RT opens your homepage. You are now signed in.

If your organization uses company sign-on, you may never see this page; you land on
your homepage directly.

To sign out when you are done, open the account menu, the main menu entry showing
your initials (or your picture once you upload one), and choose B<Logout>.

=head2 Get your bearings: the RT homepage

Your homepage is the page RT shows after login. It is built from
L<portlets|docs/glossary.pod>: small panels that each show one list or tool, such as
the tickets you own, new tickets waiting for someone to pick up, or a quick
ticket-creation form. To change what your homepage shows, open the account menu and
choose B<Settings>, then B<Home>: the Customize Home page lets you pick the
dashboard RT uses as your homepage and create dashboards of your own (see
L<Dashboards|docs/dashboards.pod>).

Three things sit at the top of every page and are worth finding now:

=over 4

=item *

The main menu: B<Home>, B<Search>, B<Reports>, and more, ending with the account

=item *

The B<Search Tickets> box toward the right. Type a ticket number to go straight to

=item *

The B<Create new ticket> button next to it. This is how you open a new request, and
it is your next step.

=back

=head2 Create a ticket

Create a ticket for any request or task you want RT to track. Requests often arrive
by email, but plenty come in by phone or in person, and then someone on the team
records them. That is what you will do now: a colleague at the front desk has asked
for a second monitor, and you will enter the request for them.

=over 4

=item 1

Click B<Create new ticket> at the top of any page. RT opens the ticket-creation form
in the default queue.

=item 2

Check the B<Queue> shown in the B<Basics> box. If you need a different queue, choose
it from the dropdown.

=item 3

Look at B<Requestors>. RT fills in your own address, assuming the ticket is for you.
This one is not, so remove your address and type the name or email address of the
person the request is for, then pick them from the suggestions RT offers.

=item 4

In B<Subject>, write a short summary of the request, for example
C<Second monitor for the front desk check-in station>. This is what people see in
ticket lists, so keep it specific.

=item 5

In the message box below, describe the request in full. This first message becomes the
start of the ticket's history.

Before you submit, know that creating the ticket sends email: RT mails the Requestor
an automatic confirmation. RT runs on email, so this is normal and predictable, not a
mistake. If you would rather not notify the Requestor this time, tick B<Do not send
email to Requestors on create>, the checkbox just under the B<Requestors> field.
(Similar checkboxes sit under B<Cc> and B<Admin Cc>.) This page does not show the full
list of who gets mailed; you will see that list on the update screen when you reply, a
little later.

=item 6

Fill in any other fields your queue requires, and leave the rest at their defaults
for now.

=item 7

Click B<Create>.

=back

RT saves the ticket and shows its display page, with a confirmation like
S<C<Ticket 5 created in queue 'General'>> at the top. The number (here, 5) is the
ticket's permanent ID. RT has also mailed the Requestor an automatic confirmation
carrying that number; the ticket's history, further down the page, records the
outgoing message.

Take a moment to read the display page. B<The Basics> shows the ticket's B<Status> (a
new ticket starts as C<new>), its queue, and its B<Owner>: the person responsible for
working it, initially C<Nobody in particular>. You will be handling this ticket, so
open B<Actions> on the page menu, the menu for the page you are on, and choose
B<Take> to make yourself the Owner.

Below that, the B<People> box lists everyone connected to the ticket, grouped by
L<role|docs/glossary.pod>: the B<Owner> working it (now you), the B<Requestors> the
ticket is for (the address you entered), and two more lists, B<Cc> (people copied on
replies) and B<AdminCc> (staff copied on everything, internal notes included).
Requestors, Ccs, and AdminCcs are the ticket's L<watchers|docs/glossary.pod>: the
people who receive email about it. Roles matter because they decide who receives
email, which the next sections put to work.

=head2 Reply to the ticket

A L<reply|docs/glossary.pod> is a message to the people the ticket is for. Replying
emails your message to the ticket's Requestors and Ccs. Use it to answer them, ask a
question, or tell them what you have done.

A reply is not the same as a L<comment|docs/glossary.pod>. A comment is an internal
working note: it is not emailed to the Requestors or Ccs. It is recorded on the ticket
(and sent to AdminCcs, if there are any). On the update screen the reply box is
shaded red and the comment box yellow, so you can tell at a glance whether your words
will leave the building.

=over 4

=item 1

On the ticket's display page, open B<Actions> on the page menu and choose
B<Reply>.

=item 2

Type your message in the box. (It is shaded red: a reminder that the Requestor will
receive it.)

=item 3

Leave B<Update Type> set to B<Reply to requestors>.

=item 4

Check the B<Scrips and Recipients> box below the message. It lists exactly who will
be emailed when you send this update, with a checkbox next to each address so you can
skip someone this one time. For this reply it lists the Requestor you entered, plus
any internal addresses your queue copies on ticket mail (its AdminCcs). One address
is missing: yours. RT does not email you your own update.

While you are here, flip B<Update Type> to B<Comments (Not sent to requestors)> and
watch the screen: the message box turns yellow, and the Requestor drops out of
B<Scrips and Recipients>, leaving only the AdminCcs. That is a comment's whole
audience. Flip back to B<Reply to requestors> and the box turns red again.

=item 5

Click B<Update Ticket>.

=back

RT returns to the display page and confirms S<C<Correspondence added>>. Your message
now appears in the ticket's history, and RT has emailed it to the Requestor. If the
ticket was still C<new>, RT also sets its status to C<open> for you, to show that
work has started.

=head2 Update the ticket as you work

Working a ticket is more than replying. As you learn more, you also change the ticket
itself. Say you want to sharpen the subject line so the ticket is easier to spot in a
list.

=over 4

=item 1

On the ticket's display page, find the B<Basics> box and click the edit pencil next to
it. RT opens the Basics for editing.

=item 2

Change the B<Subject>, for example to C<Second monitor for front desk (approved)>.

=item 3

Save your changes.

=back

RT returns to the display page showing the new subject, and records the change in the
ticket's history.

A real ticket collects many updates on its way to done: replies to the Requestor,
internal comments, status changes, and small edits like this one. RT keeps them all in
the history, so the ticket stays a full record of what happened and when.

=head2 Resolve the ticket

When the request is handled, L<resolve|docs/glossary.pod> the ticket: set its status
to C<resolved>. Resolving also emails the Requestor a short standard notice that the
request has been resolved.

=over 4

=item 1

On the ticket's display page, open the B<Actions> menu and choose B<Resolve>.

=item 2

RT opens an update screen with B<Status> already set to C<resolved>. Add a closing
note if you like: what the fix was, or anything the next person who reads this ticket
should know. The note is a comment (its box is yellow), so it stays internal; the
Requestor's resolved notice is fixed text and does not include what you type here.
The B<Scrips and Recipients> box shows the split: your note goes only to the
AdminCcs, while the Requestor is listed for the resolved notice. If you leave the
box empty, RT records just the status change and adds no comment.

=item 3

Click B<Update Ticket>.

=back

The display page returns with B<Status> now C<resolved>. You have taken a request
through its whole life in RT.

One more thing worth knowing: the B<Actions> menu changes with the ticket's status. On
the ticket you just resolved it offers B<Re-open> rather than B<Resolve>, because
re-opening is the common next step from here. The B<Actions> menu is a shortlist of
common next steps from where the ticket is now, and your administrator decides what
appears on it and how each entry is labeled. To move a ticket to a status that is not
on the menu, use the B<Status> dropdown on the update screen, which lists the statuses
the queue's L<lifecycle|docs/glossary.pod> allows from here. A lifecycle is the map of
a ticket's statuses and the steps allowed between them.

=head2 Who gets email

Email is the part of RT new users worry about most, so here is the whole story.
Notifications follow the ticket's roles, and they are predictable:

=over 4

=item *

B<When a ticket is created>, the Requestor gets an automatic reply confirming the
ticket exists and giving its number. At the same time RT notifies the ticket's Owner
and any AdminCcs and Ccs that a new ticket has arrived, so the people responsible for
it know right away. (Your walkthrough ticket did this: RT recorded the outgoing mail in
the ticket's history the moment you created it.)

=item *

B<When someone replies>, the message is emailed to the Requestors and Ccs, and a copy
goes to the Owner and AdminCcs.

=item *

B<When someone comments>, Requestors and Ccs get nothing. Only AdminCcs are notified.

=item *

B<When the ticket is resolved>, the Requestor gets the standard "your request has
been resolved" notice.

=item *

B<By default, RT does not copy you on your own updates.> You do not get a mailed copy
of a reply or comment you made yourself, since you already know what you did. That is
why your own address did not appear in the B<Scrips and Recipients> box. This is a
preference, not a fixed rule: to get copies of your own updates, open the account
menu, choose B<Settings>, then B<Preferences>, and in the B<Mail> section set
B<Outgoing mail> to B<Yes>.

=back

Before any update goes out, the B<Scrips and Recipients> box on the update screen
shows the exact recipient list, so you never have to guess. If email in your RT
behaves differently, your administrator has customized the notifications. Ask your team
how notifications are set up for your queue and why; there is usually a good reason,
tied to how the team uses RT.
